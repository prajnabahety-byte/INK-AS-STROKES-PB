🏜️ EXPLANATION

This poem paints the desert as a vast, empty expanse where silence and heat dominate. The imagery of burning sand, sweeping gales, and the absence of flora and fauna emphasizes desolation. Yet, the desert is not only a physical landscape — it is compared to ruins, places where life once thrived but has now vanished. The “request for a drop of Nile” highlights the longing for water, a symbol of vitality, which contrasts sharply with the desert’s barrenness. Just as ruins are remnants of civilizations, the desert is portrayed as a ruin of nature, stripped of abundance and authority. The fury of the burning sun becomes a destructive force, leaving the soil infertile and the land inhospitable. The poem suggests that both deserts and ruins share a haunting grandeur: they are silent witnesses to absence, marked by what is missing rather than what is present. The deeper meaning lies in this comparison — deserts are natural ruins, while ruins are human deserts, both embodying emptiness and loss. The poem invites reflection on impermanence, showing how both nature and civilization can collapse into silence. Ultimately, it is about the parallel between earthly deserts and ruins, each a reminder of fragility and transience.
